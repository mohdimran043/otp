7 · What happens to a captured frame

Nine steps, in order. The order is the design: each one exists to stop a specific failure being mistaken for a different one.

Why "store before decoding"

Every captured image is written to disk before anything tries to read it. A frame that cannot be decoded is the primary evidence for why a camera is going badly — blur, a tear, a hand across the lens — and it is the only thing a replay can work from. Discarding it would throw away the diagnosis along with the frame.

Why a failed chunk gets no reply at all

This is the part that most often surprises people, and it is deliberate. A frame that fails its checksum is not reported. There is no negative acknowledgement.

A frame that could not be read cannot say which chunk it was carrying — that information was in the part that failed to decode. So there is nothing to send a complaint about. The sender learns of the failure by its acknowledgement timing out, and shows the chunk again. Waiting for good news is the only mechanism that can work here; waiting for bad news would mean waiting for a message that can never be composed.

The acknowledgement, and why the same chunk stops appearing

When a chunk passes both checksums, the receiver writes a small signed record:

{
  "transmission_id" : "1adc37ba-fb0d-4c1d-a80e-ad62e0250924",
  "chunk_number"    : 47,
  "status"          : "ok",
  "bit_error_rate"  : 0.0,
  "received_at"     : "2026-08-07T14:33:02.881Z"
}
   + HMAC-SHA256 over the exact bytes above

The sender reads it, marks chunk 47 acknowledged, and never displays it again. Three consequences follow, and together they are the whole delivery model:

  • The display converges. As acknowledgements arrive, the set of frames still worth showing shrinks. The display spends its time on what has not arrived rather than cycling through what has.
  • The display never idles. If everything in the current window is awaiting acknowledgement, the oldest outstanding frame is shown again — a camera pointed at a blank screen learns nothing.
  • A duplicate is not acknowledged twice. The sender already stopped showing that chunk and cannot un-know it, so a second record carries no information. Suppressing them was worth 4× in wall clock: a repeated five-transfer test went from 14.5 s to 3.4 s, and its rate stopped collapsing from 192 KB/s to 24 KB/s across runs.

The acknowledgement channel is the one input the sender takes from outside itself. Every record is signed with HMAC-SHA256 over its exact bytes, using a secret both sides hold and which has no default. Anything able to write that channel could otherwise report chunks as delivered when they were not — and the sender would move on, producing a file that is quietly incomplete.
